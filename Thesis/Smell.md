The Body Social - Anthony Synnott

Anthony Synnott distinguishes between three kinds of smell - natural , manufactured and symbolic. Though these types are conceptually separate, they may simutaneously occur in a given social situation.

If the historical works on the sense of smell are accounted for, then smell or olfaction acquires a low status in the sensory hierarchy -
1. According to Aristotle, while sight an hearing were the most important sense that allowed contributions in beauty and music, and taste and touch were in the lowest rung associated with beastly nature , which alone could be abused by gluttony and lust. 
2. In his discussion on aesthetics, Kant left out olfaction completely. Thus, if Kant’s theorisation of aesthetic is followed, then there is no aesthetic of smell in the Western tradition. 
3. Though Hegel did discuss the nose and smell in his Aesthetics , but regarded it as the lowest among the four senses he use in his theory.
4. Freud was also of the opinion that smell was a characteristic animal sense. 

Synnott writes that “physical odour and the metaphysical reality are symbolically reciprocal”, therefore sense are often evaluated as positive or negative in the context in which they are remembered. Consequently , the meanings of odours are extrinsic and individually or socially constructed. Synnott thus rhetorically asks , if the nose is born or created? 

The social process of meaning making associated with the senses, like smell, transform them into a social attribute with the power to make moral statements about an individual or a group. One of the earliest observations in this regard was made by George Orwell , who pointed that the “real secret” of class distinction would be summarised in four words, “The lower classes smell”. (Synnott,194) (Classen Howes and Synnott , 8) He persisted that while race, religion , temperament can be overcome, the “physical repulsion cannot” attributing to class distinction. (Classen Howes and Synnott , 8) 

Similar distinctions along the racial lines, have been expressed in North America with regards to the olfactory presence of Blacks. Thomas Jefferson, was recorded saying that Blacks have a “a very strong and disagreeable odour”. Echoing similar prejudices, Jamaican planter Edward Long wrote in 1774, that Blacks have a “bestial or faetid” smell, which abolitionist Benjamin Rush later i 1790s, attributed to leprosy. (Synnott, 195) Similarly in Europe, this was true of Hitler who condemned the “smell of Jews” as symbolic of their moral standing. (Synnott, 196)

Scholar Gunner Myrdal talks about the tangible, social implications of olfactory beliefs , when they are employed for moral labelling, Drawing on the interaction between Blacks and Whites in North America, he observed : “The belied in a particular ‘hircine odour’ […] touches a personal sphere and is useful to justify the denial of social intercourse and the use of public convenience. (Synnott, 196) 

Social psychologist John Dollard noted in ‘Castle and Class in Southern Town’ that Whites often claimed that the Blacks had a “disagreeable, acrid ” smell which made close association impassable. This smell was ot due to living conditions but intrinsic to Blacks, “even among the cleanest of them”. Interestingly, this odour however did not prevent Whites from have the Blacks as servants. (Classen Howes and Synnott , 167) 

Thus, Synnott rightly argues that odour, whether perceived or imagined, can reinforce social hierarchies based on class and race. It can also become a means of associating particular populations with negative moral characteristics. (Synnott, 197)

In “Aroma: The cultural Smell of History” authors Constance classes, David Howes and Anthony Synnott raise importance questions about the destabilising nature of heightened olfactory consciousness in the social order of the West. They wrote that the diffusive nature of odours subvert the modern, linear worldview (with its emphasis on privacy, discrete division and superficial interaction) as it escapes and crosses boundaries amalgamating different entities into an olfactory whole.  (Classen Howes and Synnott , 6-7)

I would like to argue that , as smell cannot be contained in physical space it becomes important to interpret it and provide it with either a positive or negative value. This becomes a crucial what to maintain power structure by establishing a discourse on smell, so that interaction between different entities are limited even when there is a sense of shared olfactory whole. This can explain how the Whites continued to have Blacks in their domestic space as servants, while ensuring a metaphysical wall that kept them apart due to the repelling qualities of their odour.
