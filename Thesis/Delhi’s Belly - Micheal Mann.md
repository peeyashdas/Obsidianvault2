SUMMARY

“Old Delhi was made old through neglect and underdevelopment” - Micheal Mann

Mann’s article discusses the development of the water and sewage system in colonial Delhi. What I reckoned from his article is that Delhi was not one fixed geographical space , and neither a unitary urban environment. This he demonstrated through the changing water and sewage system , which may appear as part of the urban planning, but had a long term effect on the environment of Delhi. In fact, the idea of old Delhi and New Delhi may as well be traced to the urban decay and development in the respective areas. How did this striking difference come to be?

Through his discussion on the water work and drainage system, Mann demonstrated how efforts by colonial administration was driven by racial and segregational (bio)politics, whereby the efforts to maintain and salvage precolonial systems were made only as long their was physical proximity between the native and the white population. However, by the early twentieth century with the creation of the imperial capital, financial resources were directed towards the development and maintenance  of New Delhi as the cost of the walled city that came to be dubbed as Old Delhi. Eventually , the  Old City became the “picturesque” Oriental background of the modern Western New Delhi, serving as a lesson for the importance of colonial rule as instrument of civic enhancement. 

This spatial distinction did not occur overnight, and Mann highlights this by focusing his study on the 1800s, where efforts were made at salvaging the water works and sewerage systems  through patchwork across Delhi (old and new) amidst financial conservatism, exposing the maladministration by local authorities and preferential treatment of the white areas like Civil Lines , that began to plague the urban planning of Delhi. 

Mann’s work provides an analytical link between the spring mosquito nuisance and its causal explanation provided by Col. Covell in the press note itself.  I intend to use his work to support my premise of segregational urban planning that was operating in Delhi, and how it not only failed but contributed to the problem on spring mosquito nuisance.

While the 18th century discourse on sanitation in town was centred around the removal of pathogenic environments (like drainage of swamps, ventilation of closed urban areas and burying of corpses outside the cities), the 19th century saw the ‘cleanliness measures’ being extended to excrement and refuse remove. The epidemiological discourse was expanding, with miasma theory and the contagion theory directing policy making. However, these policies were not objective decisions but rather governed by imperial philosophy social Darwinism and thus, racial superiority. “When the first report on the sanitary condition of the Punjab was published in 1867, Delhi was described as the cleanest city of the province, even in the bylanes and back alleys. The removal of refuse and excreta was organized by the city wards (mohalla). In every mohalla there was a masonry receptacle with or without a lid. “But with respect to drainage the same report stated that ‘superficially the city presents an appearance of cleanliness not often seen in the cities of the Punjab, but it is undermined with cess pits whose foul contents poison the water by percolation and the air by evaporation and make the place pestilential.

Echoing the European sanitation discourse, the report concluded that sufficient water supply would substantially improve public health in Delhi. With respect to the sewerage system the report recommended that covered sewers constructed on the English model would be infinitely preferable to any Indian construction and also be less expensive.

Arguments regarding the dangers of disease deriving from polluted rivers, which were part of the European sanitary discourse, were denied as having any applicability to India from the very beginning. Furthermore, the argument certainly made cheap solutions for sewerage systems in British India possible. Emptying sewerage into the Jamuna saved money. For this reason the DMC was able to spend little money on the maintenance of the existing system. By 1871, just nine new drains had been built and twenty repaired.”
