On Sanitary Landfills 

Prasad writes that when the idea of nightsoil disposal out the city drew the attention of  DMC , it followed the sweepers to Malkaganj , where they disposed the waste by pitting, which came to be known as sanitary landfilling in the 1920s. The process involved the layering of garbage with ashes and street sweeping. The heap was then packed in place by applying pressure and covered with leaves to prevent the spreading of the refuse. This was a labour intensive process that required large tracts of surplus land. Before the expansion of Delhi, sites including Malkaganj (northwest), Delhi Gate (south), Motia Khan and Original Road (west) around the walled city was used for this purpose.

However, in the aftermath of the Delhi Sore of 1858 and the continuous expansion of the colonial settlement, the British reconsidered the locations of these sanitary landfills. For the colonial administration, it was importance that the landfills were located away from the European residential areas and military encampment, as they carried sensory discomfort and contamination into the white spaces.  Malkaganj located in the west of Civil Lines was one of the major concerns, as the westerlies in summer carried with it unpleasant smells into the European clubs and residences. Finally, in 1893, Chief Commissioner J. H. Grey proposed a location of the landfill to the south of the walled city. This ensured that Shahjahanabad acted as a buffer zone between the Civil Lines and the disposal site. Thus, the Delhi Gate Dump came into existence. 

The main subsoil drain opened at the outskirts of Delhi Gate, along the southern edge of the walled city. Its effluent was reused as sullage water, while insoluble waste was buried at the landfill. Delhi Gate was considered a convenient site for waste disposal because of its proximity to the drainage outlet, the availability of land for refuse disposal, and its distance from the Civil Lines. The colonial administration also preferred disposal sites where prevailing winds would carry unpleasant smells away from European residential settlements. Similarly, the authorities stipulated that waste dumped into the river should be discharged downstream of the Fort, rather than near the Bathing Ghat and Water Bastion.

It is needless to say, that ensuring pleasant sensorial experience of the European population came at the cost of the native population, as the DMC consequently located refuse disposal grounds alongside native residential areas. The authorities assumed that the “natives were immune to refuse, even fond of offal.”

By the 1920s, however, the Delhi Gate dump presented a sanitary problem due to its proximity to the newly constructed capital. The Jail Superintendent of the Delhi Jail located at the immediate south of the dump became the keenest critique of the dumping ground, raising concerns about proliferation of flies and miasma. 

Although germ theory had gained prominence, colonial officials continued to associate unpleasant smells with the potential spread of disease. Refuse dumps were considered hazardous because of the accumulation of organic matter, which decomposed and putrefied, particularly during the rainy season. Flies, in turn, were regarded as indicators of insanitary conditions and potential disease outbreaks. During outbreaks of flies, official attention repeatedly turned to the nightsoil and refuse disposal grounds at Delhi Gate and Motia Khan.
