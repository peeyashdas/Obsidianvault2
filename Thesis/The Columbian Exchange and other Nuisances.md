The idea of this paper stems from a press note published in 1938, concerning the spring mosquito nuisance in Delhi. According to this report the spring mosquito nuisance was an annual phenomenon from the month of March to April that affected the regions in and around Delhi. This press note majorly expresses its discomfort with the presence of these mosquitos that affected the urban population of Delhi as their bites were supposed to cause bodily discomfort , even if they did not carry any disease causing pathogens. 

The central research question of my paper investigates how the problem of the "spring mosquito nuisance" complicates the idea of spatial segregation and sanitary control in colonial India. While colonial urban planning sought to maintain a distinction between the "clean" White spaces and the supposedly "dirty" native quarters, the problem of mosquito exceeded these segregated boundaries. Unlike walls or municipal regulations, mosquitos could move freely across urban spaces, carrying contamination beyond the limits of racial segregation. The sensorial dimension of the fly nuisance that is its buzzing presence, visibility, and association with contamination makes it an important subject for understanding colonial anxieties surrounding hygiene and disease.

The paper also discusses the anti - malaria policies legislated by the government and their failure in containing the problem of spring mosquitos and in some instances, malaria itself. Through this endeavour the paper aims to reevaluate Alfred Crosby’s conception of ecological imperialism that focuses on the asymmetrical impact of biological exchange between the European colonisers and the native settlers. Contrarily, I wish to argue that colonial ecological imperialism was not absolute, as the colonial administration repeatedly struggled to contain contamination even within European residential spaces. The persistence of flies and disease demonstrates the limitations of colonial sanitary control and destabilises the notion that racial segregation could produce hygienically secure urban environments.

There are relatively few scholarly works that directly address the issue of the spring mosquito nuisance in colonial Delhi. However, there exists a broader body of scholarship on anti-malaria campaigns, sanitary reforms, and public health policies undertaken by the British colonial administration. This study aims to build upon that scholarship while drawing on archival sources available through the National Archives of India, including reports on the spring mosquito and Legislative Assembly discussions on sanitation and public health from late 19th century till independence. Moreover, I will consult municipal records, newspapers, and medical reports to analyse how sanitation, contamination, and disease figured in administrative discussions and in relation to  public discourse. 

At length I wish to argue that much of the image of the sanitised White colonial city was constructed through administrative narratives, literature, and media representations, while in practice racial segregation could not fully control ecological processes or disease transmission.

## Main Argument 

Ecological imperialism is a key historical concept that links ecological transformation with imperialist (and in extension colonial) developments from 15th century onwards to explain the rise of European powers and their domination over the non - European world. 
The concept was further cemented by Alfred Crosby, in his seminal work The Columbian Exchange, that refers to the large scale exchange of flora and fauna between the Old World and the New World that followed the arrival of Columbus in Bahamas in 1492. However, this exchange was rather favourable to the Europeans who carried not only flora and fauna but diseases that led to large scale extermination of the indigenous population, leaving a potent void suitable for settler colonialism , in many parts of the world. 

This study that derives its theoretical framework from ecological imperialism, goes onto invert this concept to argue that at provincial levels the ecology of the colonies dominated the experience of the colonial settlers. The study aims to achieve this through two case studies of  malaria and spring mosquito in Delhi. 

The term ecology here being interpreted not just as the physical component that constitutes the biotic and abiotic component, but also the space where sensory experiences are occurring. 

Indeed the ecological imperialism, facilitated through the columbian exchange greatly altered the biological, geographical and social component of the new world, creating what Crosby called, neo - Europes. However, I wish to argue that investigation beyond the early years of the voyages of discovery, would reveal the micro histories of environmental transformation in the colonies that subsequently posed challenges and resistance to colonial power and administration.
