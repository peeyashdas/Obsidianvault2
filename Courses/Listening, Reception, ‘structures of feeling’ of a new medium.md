Introduction

"From a distance, you can already hear the funeral music preceding the coffin. The head of the procession has just turned from Unter den Linden into Wilhelmstrasse, and the first mounted police officers are already approaching the Reich President's Palace."

Imagine a middle-class German household gathered in the sitting room, united in grief at the death of their leading statesman. From the wooden radio set, a distant voice carries the family into the funeral procession, while the murmurs of the crowd and strains of music seep through the pauses in the broadcast. For a moment, the tragedy unfolding beyond their walls becomes almost tangible, bringing a distant public event into the intimacy of the home. 

Sound has a connective power of its own; travelling across distances and penetrating physical boundaries. Thus, when the first radio broadcasting was set up in the post - 1919 Germany, it set in motion the nationalisation of the public culture, permeating the German society and reaching a climax in 1933. . 

Historian Karl Christian Führer, warned against a teleological approach in analysing the social history of Weimar broadcasting. While most scholarly works develop an argument along the idea of a “mass culture” that was forged as an outcome of the Weimar broadcasting, a critical analysis of this history reveals a more complicated development. 

Thus, this essay delves into the larger architecture of radio in the Weimar Republic, and investigates how it passed onto the Third Reich as a tool of propaganda. Moreover, the essay will discuss the ‘structures of feeling’ of a new medium and the inherent capacity of radio as a political agent.

Organisation

  

In the early 1920s, the Weimar broadcasting was a conglomeration of nine companies that operated in designated regions of the Reich, with the exception of Deutsche Welle. This setup was formalised on 15 May 1925, with the establishment of the Reichs-Rundfunk-Gesellschaft (RRG or Reich Broadcasting Corporation) as the parent company , in Berlin. According to Führer, these companies were not part of a free market economy, and the broadcasting was tightly controlled through "public concession” and licensing. 

Figure 1. Heinrich Giesecke, Entwicklung und Aufbau des Deutschen Rundfunks, 1930. Reproduced in 'A Medium of Modernity? Broadcasting in Weimar Germany 1923–1932’by Karl Christian Führer, 1997, The Journal of Modern History 69, no. 4, pp 722–53.

Interestingly , these policies of the Reich were far from affecting a national culture. In granting the operating licenses, the postal ministry endeavored to demarcate the radio broadcasting from the existing media, the print and motion pictures, which were governed by party politics and international influence. In an attempt to protect the sovereignty of the broadcasting medium and keep it free from “sordid competition” the Reich granted each company monopoly over a particular area of Germany.  

Paradoxically, the major aim of Reichs’ policies regarding the Weimar broadcasting was to establish a decentralised German broadcasting, that would be focusing on strengthening regional culture and community. However, the structure that it adopted was intensely conservative and susceptible to centralisation, as became the case in the early years of the National Socialist party, controlled by the Berlin ministry of propaganda. 

However, this was not the case in the early years, as Hans Bredow took the office of Reichs Rundfunk Kommissar, in June 1926. By this time, the Reichpost had acquired 51 percent of the RRG and established virtual control over the Weimar broadcasting. Nonetheless, Bredow believed in the acculturating power of the radio, beyond its capitalist function:

“Radio should help Germany maintain its endangered status as a Kulturnation: Love of the arts, public spirit, and thirst for knowledge come to life again and create the basis for intellectual sowing and ripening. . . . We will and must preserve our intellectual level [geistige Hohe]. While foreign nations strive for a wider range [of transmission stations], we strive for deepening [Vertiefung].”

A broad array of programmes were thus curated for people ranging from operas to lectures. Although Bredow conceived of radio as an apolitical medium for spreading culture and serving the interests of everyone, its programmes were particularly directed towards the lower classes, who were still seen as needing integration into the German Kulturnation. 

Reception and Structures of Feeling

  

For Karl Christian Führer, the questions of reception were as important as the transmission. The popularity of the radio medium can easily be gauged from the rising subscriptions despite the financial instability of the nation characterized by widespread loss of savings, persistent job insecurity, severe government austerity, and hyperinflation.

Moreover, he raised the important question of how the new listeners experienced radio. Führer’s study of the radio listeners were not restricted to simple statistical figures, as he argued that radios were testimonials of social history, exemplified through a photograph of a Christmas celebration in a German household in mid - 1920s. The complexity of the radio models prior to 1926 - 27, brought him to the conclusion that “broadcasting (was) a very masculine medium during the first years of its existence”.

In the course of technological advancement, with wooden encasing and integrated loudspeaker, the radio transformed from an awe-inspiring technical miracle to a part of everyday life. Besides, the loudspeaker enabled radio to be experienced collectively. With the ease in operation introduced around 1928-29, the initial fascination with the radio was replaced by greater attention to its content. 

  

Consequently, many records of the Reichs Rundfunk Gesellschaft archived in the Federal Archives of Germany, are dedicated to statistics on reception and discussions regarding listening.  Following are three examples to elaborate on the point. 

  

1. International Broadcasting Union

  

The Union Internationale de Radiophonie or the International Broadcasting Union was an initiative of European radio broadcasters , established in April 1925. It resulted from the International Telegraph Union and League of Nations' decision to avoid regulating radio as they perceived it as a nascent technology. The main goal of the Union was to act as an arbitrator in case of disputes. Moreover, it organised international conferences and as of late 1920s, the member countries fostered exchange of radio programmes. The Union had its headquarters in Geneva , Switzerland and many operational records of the association are in French. The association formed of about twenty - six (estimated) private broadcasting companies and the British Broadcasting Corporation, gave importance to the radio reception, and had a technical office dedicated to measuring user frequency in Brussels. 

  

Interestingly, monthly reports from the International Broadcasting Association used to be  translated and posted by the RRG to the Reichspost regularly. These reports covered the developments in radio broadcasting across the world and presented the statistical data on reception, calculated every month. Additionally, it presented detailed reports on the activities of the RRG. ( Fig. 2)

2. Hans Flesch and the Horspiel

  

The importance of listening was not restricted to statistical data, but there were genuine efforts at improving the quality of content produced. Dr. Hans Flesch was a pioneer in radio in Weimar Germany, who gained popularity as a radio broadcaster and Horspiel writer, authoring the first ever radio play produced in German titled “Magic on the Air”. On 24 October 1924, Southwest German Radio broadcast the performance from Frankfurt, where Flesch worked as artistic director. Since live broadcasts could not be recorded at the time, the performance survived only in a written version published shortly afterwards. 

According to Lecia Rosenthal, Magic on the Air explored disorder in broadcasting through unscripted, unauthorized, and unexpected access to the radio apparatus. The play reflected contemporary debates about how radio should be defined and regulated, contrasting the predictable and scheduled nature of broadcasting with the possibility of radio as an uncontrolled and unfamiliar form of expression. Rosenthal argued that Flesch used the Horspiel to explore what made radio distinct from other art forms and how a specifically radio-based artistic language could develop.

Moreover, Magic on the Air explored the distinctive qualities of radio as a medium. Flesch described the play as an attempt to develop an art form specific to radio. He presented the work as an unfinished experiment that sought to define radio through the adaptation of existing aesthetic forms. For long Flesch was involved in the radioplay genre, and emphasised on the importance of listening through speeches and presentations. 

In the monthly report from October 1930 of the International Broadcasting Union, the developments in the International Theatre Congress organised in July 1930, in Hamburg are reproduced. This section details the presentation given by Dr.  Flesch , at the time, the director of the Funkstunde Berlin (Berlin Radio Hour) on “Rundfunk und Horspiel” (Radio and Radioplay). He underlined the importance of radio as a medium of artistic production, besides its function of amplification and transmission of information, and reproduction of music , literature , etc for public education. For Flesch, the radio artwork meant a piece of work written and produced only through it. In his speech, where he categorised  reproduction from production in radio broadcasting, he placed radioplay in the latter category. 

According to Flesch, in the early years, radio plays followed the model of theatre. It tried to compensate for the absence of visual elements through sound. He argued though radio had begun to develop its own form of drama, yet no fully satisfactory work had yet been produced. He also pointed out the technical and financial difficulties involved in producing radio plays. He emphasised that radio needed playwrights who could create works specifically for the medium. Radio drama, therefore, had to develop its own path instead of constantly borrowing from theatre.

The radioplay as a radio specific production ,thus, was of great importance in the European broadcasting network. Away from the didactic nature of lectures, radio plays had the unique capacity of stirring the deepest feelings of the human heart. The unique nature of Horspie, to mobilize the sentiments of the people, was often used to achieve political objectives. Unlike speeches, the plays could very well communicate a specific message without explicitly voicing it. One such play produced in the 1930s, was Der Marsch zum Salzmeer, written by Soumendranath Tagore and Felix Gasbarra, that managed to stir up a conflict of interest between the German state bureaucracy and the British colonial administration in India. The reason behind this was the anti - British content of the play. The original script of the play has yet to be found, however, a series of letters exchanged between the British Embassy in Berlin and  British offices in India , have reproduced the content of play. The play provides an important case study of how the idea of educating the public was reinterpreted in response to the needs of the time. 

Understanding the Listener: The Limits of Weimar Broadcasting 

As established in the previous sections, the Reichspost exercised strict control over radio broadcasting through the introduction of licences and the acquisition of significant shares in the broadcasting companies, which gave it a decisive role in administrative decision-making. However, this control extended beyond the production side of radio to the regulation of listening. It authorised licences for the installation and use of wireless receivers and went further by introducing a monthly subscription fee of RM 2 for their renewal. From the outset of broadcasting, it was also involved in the curation of programmes. These measures were symptomatic of the Reichspost’s shortsightedness, as they increasingly made broadcasting susceptible to power and political control, precisely what the institution had sought to prevent. The concentration of radio as an instrument of political propaganda under the Nazi regime thus had its beginnings in the administrative policies of the Reichspost.

Führer’s study reveals that the resistance to the license and subscription from the radio listeners were passive but influential. The study of the subscription patterns and cancellation rate in the Bavarian region, indicated a progressive cancelling rate, which was initially obscured by the overall rise in the subscription fuelled by the new listeners. However, the sustained economic strains in the later half of the 1920s, could no longer sustain this progress.  

  

Prior to the Great Depression, “lack of time and interest” was the most significant reason for the cancellations making up 30.8% of all cancellations in 1927. The Weimar broadcasting was limited in variety and there were little to no choice of stations. When radio executives were offered the option of broadcasting more than a single programme, protestations were verbalised virulently. The listeners , on the other hand,  actively resisted the cultural ambition of the radio executives by tuning out, switching off the radio or cancelling the subscription, if the radio programmes were lecture or classical music heavy.  A survey of  1933-34, revealed that taste for classical artforms were class - based. While a staggering proportion of upper class civil servants and professionals expressed a liking for classical concerts, only about 8 percent of unskilled and 11 percent of skilled workers felt alike. 

  

During the years of the Great Depression, the economic burden of the radio license and subscription became the primary cause for cancellations, attributing 41.7 percent in 1931 compared to 24.4 percent in 1927. Moreover, Führer calls the Weimar radio an urban medium, where technical limitations and high cost of radio sets contributed to the rural - urban divide. In 1932, while 46 percent of urban families had a radio, radio ownership in rural areas was 7.9 percent. Consequently, far from embodying “Bildungsoptimismus,” listeners practised an everyday form of resistance through selective listening.

  

Into the Third Reich

  

A meticulous study on the role of mass media in Weimar Germany and the Third Reich demonstrates that radio influenced both the preservation and erosion of democratic institutions. 

  

According to this study, the content of radio broadcasting in pre-World War II Germany underwent two significant changes. Prior to 1929, Weimar broadcasting primarily produced cultural and educational content, while Drahtloser Dienst AG was the only broadcasting company to monopolise political news. Between 1924 and 1925, the role of political broadcasting on the radio was the subject of considerable debate, which eventually led to an official ban on political content in 1926. From 1926 to 1929, radio broadcasting remained intentionally apolitical, with limited government involvement. This, however, changed with the rise of the Young Plan campaign, which called for a referendum to reject Germany’s reparations obligations under the Treaty of Versailles. The campaign was led by the National Socialist German Workers’ Party (Nazi Party) in cooperation with other right-wing parties.

  

The Social Democratic Party (SPD), which was becoming increasingly unpopular in the context of the Great Depression, felt deeply threatened by the referendum and launched an intensive effort against it, ultimately using radio to promote its position. Although the study shows that the SPD’s intervention was pro-democratic, it nevertheless succumbed to political pressures and contributed to the transformation of radio into a propaganda medium. This marked a watershed moment in the history of broadcasting, as it paved the way for the subsequent use of radio by the Nazis. 

  

By 1932, Weimar radio had become heavily centralised and was placed under the Ministry of the Interior. Until 1933, the Weimar government had largely succeeded in keeping the Nazi Party out of radio, allowing it little to no space in broadcasting schedules. However, this changed after Hitler assumed the office of Chancellor, when the Nazi Party took control of broadcasting and used it to disseminate its political message. During the five-week period preceding the 1933 elections, Hitler, who had previously been denied access to radio, appeared on the medium eighteen times.

  

As most of the Nazi campaign of 1933 elections was aimed at uneducated workers, this was a fresh approach to broadcasting in comparison to the Weimar Reichspost which was indifferent to the preferences of the masses. This may have contributed to the growing appeal of the Nazi Party, and their victory in the March 1933 elections. 

The major contribution of this study to our understanding of the relationship between radio and the rise of the Nazi Party was its demonstration of a positive correlation between radio content and federal election results over time. The study further showed that the frequency of political figures’ appearances on radio was positively correlated with election results, highlighting the potential significance of radio exposure in shaping electoral outcomes. Conclusion

The history of broadcasting in Weimar Germany thus reveals a paradox. Although government policies increasingly brought radio under state control, it  ultimately failed to fulfil the ambition of “raising the German working class on a cultural diet.” A significant portion of the radio programme was devoted to the liberal arts, reflecting an effort “to influence the Zeitgeist.” Yet this objective was pursued through an undemocratic system in which decision-making was concentrated in the hands of a small number of individuals. 

The Weimar Reichspost adopted a teleological and paternalistic approach to governing radio broadcasting, which ultimately weakened its administrative framework. This weakening enabled the National Socialist Party to turn radio into an instrument of propaganda by dissolving its remaining democratic aspects. The political agency of radio lay in its ability to reproduce the sounds of the German political landscape. While it brought the democratic ethos of the Weimar government through vigorous speeches of the political leaders, it also brought the fascist bellicosity of the Third Reich into people’s homes, demonstrating the transformative power of sound.
