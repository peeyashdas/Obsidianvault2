#### Materiality

or this journal entry on the theme of materiality I wish to discuss the game world (cityscape, home, diet and health, ) and resources, human resource, the market  in Guild II : Renaissance

The two components of Guild II that I am focusing to analyse the defensibility of materiality in the game are - social hierarchy in the game-world and the resources in ARTO.


A major critique of the world building is the horizontal classification of the professions instead of a vertical classification. Drawing from Dyer’s model of social hierarchy, it can be argued that not every profession in the medieval society occupied the same social strata. According to Dyer’s analysis of England in the later Middle Ages, the merchant was the most important member of the society, followed by craftsmen (master, journeymen and apprentice) , labourers and servants, and finally, the marginals , that constituted the beggars, vagrants and prostitutes. Given that my game world is set in Cologne, Germany, certain aspects of the hierarchy may be different , however, it is highly unlikely the German society in the Middle Ages was devoid of inequalities. At the onset, the playing agent is required to select a career and play as either a patron, craftsman, scholar or rogue. These occupations are designed in a linear manner where there is no difference in the access to society between a patron or a rogue. Though the marketplace acts as a place of exchange of goods, the game design irons out the unevenness in these relations that existed in the later - Middle Ages. The game design overlooks the asymmetry that may exist between the patron-client relationship in the market. 

However, the game has introduced the conception of inequality and social hierarchy , through the Title feature. These titles are similar to the levels of a game, unlocking which provides access  to a wider variety of ARTO and action - choices. The design choice of buying titles holds little historical accuracy , yet, the idea that people occupied different position in the society and every position came with a degree of access to the urban space is profound. The titles which can be bought from the council house, demonstrates the idea that social mobility could be achieved through personal wealth. For example, the character starts with a Commoner status with a hut and a building limit of one. By the fifth title, the character has acquired the status of a free citizen, with privileges like henchmen, a gabled house, right to insult someone, access to higher council office and a building limit of ten. This gives a clear idea of how wealth could influence the experiences of a town person in the Middle Ages, as argued by Keith Lilley. 

Therefore, Guild II is able to enforce the materiality of the social landscape in the urban society of Middle Ages. 

Remarkably, the game world of Guild II , includes playing and non - playing agents from every rank of the society, and goes on to visibilize the marginals as non - playing agents. These marginals that include , vagrants, criminals and prostitutes, are part of the game world and the soundscape of Guild II, where first person perspective of the town gives the player the glimpse into the daily interactions between the townspeople. Occurrences of robbery, murder and seduction, give visibility to the marginals. Moreover , these features are not simple visual elements but have interactive capabilities where the playing agent may be attacked or seduced by the non - playing agents, giving the latter a substantial degree of agency. 

One of the earliest observations from the gameplay, was how the market prices responded to demand and supply economy. This steered me to look deeper into how the market operates in the Guild II. Each product in the market has a fixed base price, however, the prices are dynamic and often fall, if too many of the same products are sold in the market. This design function of the market is realistic as it operates on the economic principle of demand and supply, and it also motivates the player to produce a variety of goods to maintain the prices. This is achieved by the game design where a record for each good entering and leaving the market is kept , visible in numbers beside each good. Interestingly, certain goods (eg. cake), also hold social value as they can be used to improve interpersonal relations between the characters. The gifting system in Guild II adds a layer of depth to the resources as they have an employability in enhancing one’s reputation and improve interpersonal relations. In fact, there are clear distinction between goods that have an economic value as raw materials , while goods that have social and political value as gifts. This gives the resources a qualitative attribute as they are not only economic currency in the game, but something the playing agents can buy from the markets for their personal use. 



In addition to the market economy that governs the relations of exchange in Guild II, it also emulates the regulative aspect of the medieval markets.

Human resource and labour

Labour is unlimited in Guild II; which overlooks one of the most important resources in the Middle Ages - the human resource.With every production unit comes with a labourer at a price. These labourers are have a range of action - choices related to the production unit they are working in. For example, employees in the tavern can serve guests besides producing edibles in the kitchen. The henchman has a variety of tasks from intimidation to asking around people for information about the rival dynasty. The labourers cease to work at night, and attend church on regular basis. However, once employed these labourers have no metabolic needs or desires. They occasionally fall sick, however, while I was managing the tavern, neither of them died. 

 I reckon that materiality has been quite literally discerned in the game design, where limited attention is paid to people as resources. Why should we consider this a substantial oversight? The game places raw material at the heart of the production process , and the exhaustive nature of raw materials act as a challenge in the game play. However, as a game set in the aftermath of the Black Plague, it does not give enough historical credit to human labour , as there are considerable simplifications with the employees. Indeed , Wainwright is correct in his observation that accurate economic simulation comes with the burden of complex gameplay, risking inaccessibility. 

However, I disagree with the need to dilute the complexity of games whose core gameplay revolves around strategy and resource management.
