#### The Annales School (Founded in 1929)

The Annales School challenged traditional history writing, which often concentrated on political events, rulers, wars, and important individuals. It encouraged historians to study the deeper structures that shaped societies over time.

#### Key characteristics

- **A different understanding of time:** The past is multilayered. Events are only the visible surface of history, beneath which lie deeper structures.
    
- **Interdisciplinary approach:** Historians draw on other social sciences to understand historical change.
    
- **Beyond conventional political history:** Greater attention is given to geography, society, and long-term developments.
    
- **Movement away from Marxist theory:** The lecture identifies the Annales School's development as a path away from Marxist theory, although it shares certain interests with Marxist historians.
    

#### Strengths

1. **Long-term historical perspective:** It looks beyond individual events to the structures that shape historical developments.
    
2. **Interdisciplinary research:** It opens historical research to methods and insights from other social sciences.
    
3. **A broader understanding of the past:** It encourages historians to investigate the social and geographical conditions underlying historical events.
    

#### Limitations

- **Bias:** Historical interpretations may reflect the historian's assumptions and perspective.
    
- **Positivist stance:** The lecture raises questions about the school's commitment to positivism.
    
- **Ambivalence towards the establishment:** Its relationship with established intellectual and institutional traditions is not straightforward.
    
- **Unclear position of culture:** The role of culture in Annales history is not always clearly defined.
    

#### Fernand Braudel and the _longue durée_

Braudel's _The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II_ (1949) is a key work associated with the Annales School.

The concept of the **longue durée** refers to the long-term structures and conditions that shape historical life. Instead of focusing exclusively on individual events, historians examine the deeper patterns that persist over extended periods.

Braudel also discusses Karl Marx's historical models in _On History_:

> “Marx’s genius, the secret of his long sway lies in the fact that he was the first one to construct true social modes, on the basis of a historical longue durée. These models have been frozen in all their simplicity by being given the status of laws” (p. 51).

**Main point:** Braudel recognises the importance of Marx's long-term social analysis but criticises the tendency to treat historical models as fixed laws.

#### Annales and British Marxist historians: similarities and differences

Both approaches shared an interest in:

- Moving away from conventional political history.
    
- Investigating the structures beneath the surface of society.
    
- Developing a form of **total history**.
    
- Studying ordinary people.
    

However, there were important differences:

|Annales School|British Marxist historians|
|---|---|
|Greater interest in geography and regional histories.|Greater emphasis on class and social relations within a Marxist framework.|
|More eclectic in its theoretical approach.|More explicitly concerned with Marxist historical analysis.|
|Resisted treating historical patterns as fixed laws.|Drew on Marxist concepts to analyse historical change.|
|Less Eurocentric, according to the lecture.|Shared some concerns with Annales but followed a different theoretical tradition.|

**Remember:** Both approaches broadened the scope of historical inquiry beyond political events, but they differed in their theoretical orientations and emphases.

#### What Happened After the Annales School?

The lecture identifies several developments that followed the Annales School:

- **Borrowing:** Historians continued to borrow methods and ideas from other disciplines.
    
- **Quantification:** The use of numerical data and statistical methods became more prominent.
    
- **Rebellion:** Historians challenged established historical approaches.
    
- **From the collective to the individual:** Greater attention was given to individual experiences alongside collective social structures.
    
- **Mentalités and cultural history:** Historians increasingly investigated collective attitudes, beliefs, and cultural practices.
    
- **Short-term perspectives:** Attention to longer-term structures was complemented by studies of shorter periods.
    

**Key takeaway:** Historical scholarship did not simply abandon the Annales approach. It developed in multiple directions, incorporating new methods and questioning earlier assumptions.

#### British Marxist Historians (1950s–1960s)

British Marxist historians used Marxist ideas to examine social structures, class relations, and historical change. Rather than treating political events as the primary subject of history, they investigated how economic and social relations shaped people's lives.

The lecture focuses particularly on **E. P. Thompson**, **Antonio Gramsci**, and **E. J. Hobsbawm**.

#### Edward Palmer Thompson (1924–1993)

Thompson was a British historian best known for _The Making of the English Working Class_ (1963).

#### A. _The Making of the English Working Class_ (1963)

Thompson sought to recover the experiences of ordinary people whose lives and struggles had often been overlooked by conventional historical narratives.

He writes:

> “I am seeking to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the ‘obsolete’ hand-loom weaver, the ‘utopian’ artisan [...] from the enormous condescension of posterity.” (_The Making of the English Working Class_, p. 12)

Thompson argues that people should not be judged solely through the perspective of later generations. Their actions and aspirations must be understood within the historical circumstances in which they lived.

**Key arguments:**

1. **History from below:** Thompson gives historical importance to ordinary workers rather than concentrating exclusively on political elites.
    
2. **Subjective experience:** He examines how people experienced social and economic transformations.
    
3. **Class formation:** The working class was not simply created by industrialisation; workers participated in the process of becoming a class.
    
4. **Agency:** Workers were active participants in historical change, not merely passive victims of economic forces.
    
5. **Cultural and economic history:** Economic transformations cannot be separated entirely from cultural practices and experiences.
    

Thompson's argument that the working class was **'present at its own making'** emphasises that class formation was a historical process involving human action and experience.

#### B. _Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism_ (1967)

In this article, published in _Past & Present_, Thompson examines the relationship between industrial capitalism, time, and working habits.

The lecture raises four central questions about this text:

- Can the economic and cultural dimensions of Thompson's argument actually be separated?
    
- To what extent was industrial work discipline imposed from above?
    
- To what extent did workers participate in creating or appropriating this discipline?
    
- How should industrialisation be studied historically?
    

#### Saint Monday

**Saint Monday** refers to the custom among some workers of taking Monday off, often extending the weekend.

Its significance lies in the contrast between traditional working habits and the demands of industrial capitalism.

Industrial capitalism required greater regularity, punctuality, and discipline. Traditional practices such as Saint Monday could conflict with these demands.

Thompson's approach encourages historians to examine how established working habits were challenged and how workers responded to these changes.

#### The relationship between economic growth and culture

Thompson writes:

> “For there is no such thing as economic growth which is not, at the same time, growth or change of a culture” (p. 94).

This statement challenges the idea that economic change can be understood independently of culture.

Industrialisation transformed not only economic production but also working habits, social relationships, and people's understanding of time.

**Key takeaway:** Industrialisation should not be understood exclusively as an economic transformation. It also involved changes in culture and everyday life, and workers responded to these changes in different ways.

#### 4. Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937)

Antonio Gramsci was an Italian Marxist thinker and one of the founders of the Italian Communist Party in 1921.

#### Historical background

- Born in 1891.
    
- Helped found the Italian Communist Party in 1921.
    
- Arrested by Mussolini's government in 1926.
    
- Remained imprisoned until 1934.
    
- Died in 1937.
    
- His writings were translated in the late 1950s and subsequently influenced British Marxist historians.
    

Gramsci developed important ideas about culture, power, and the working class.

#### Cultural hegemony

**Hegemony** concerns the way dominant groups maintain their position not simply through force, but also through cultural and ideological influence.

Cultural hegemony helps explain how the values and assumptions of dominant groups can become accepted as normal or legitimate within society.

This concept broadens the analysis of power beyond direct political control or economic domination.

#### Working-class oppositional consciousness

Gramsci's ideas also draw attention to the possibility of resistance to dominant ideas.

The working class is not necessarily confined to accepting the worldview of dominant groups. It can develop an oppositional consciousness through which it challenges established social and cultural arrangements.

#### Gramsci and Edward Said

The lecture includes Gramsci in connection with Edward Said's _Orientalism_ (1976, pp. 6–7).

This connection is significant to the lecture's wider concern with the relationship between historical interpretation, culture, and power.

**Key takeaway:** Gramsci's concept of cultural hegemony helps historians examine how power operates through ideas and cultural assumptions, as well as through economic and political structures.

#### 5. The Significance of 1968

The lecture presents 1968 as an important moment of political, social, and intellectual transformation.

#### Major developments

- **Greater access to universities:** Higher education became accessible to a wider section of society.
    
- **Questioning established learning practices:** Traditional approaches to education and knowledge came under scrutiny.
    
- **More fluid class identification:** Existing assumptions about class identity were increasingly questioned.
    
- **Feminism and liberation movements:** Movements challenged established social hierarchies and inequalities.
    
- **Third-Worldism:** Political and intellectual attention increasingly focused on the experiences and struggles of societies outside the Western industrialised world.
    

#### Contradictions of 1968

The lecture also identifies several contradictions:

- Repression continued alongside movements for liberation.
    
- Idealism and individualism existed alongside collective political struggles.
    
- Calls for social transformation coexisted with established structures of power.
    

**Historical significance:** The developments associated with 1968 contributed to a broader questioning of established authority and conventional ways of understanding society. They also formed part of the wider intellectual context in which historians reconsidered whose experiences mattered and how history should be written.

#### 6. E. J. Hobsbawm: From Social History to the History of Society

E. J. Hobsbawm's article _From Social History to the History of Society_ (1971), published in _Daedalus_, examines the development of social history and its relationship to broader approaches to historical explanation.

#### Social history versus the history of society

The distinction suggested by the title raises an important question: is social history simply the study of particular social groups and subjects, or should it aim to explain how society as a whole operates and changes?

Hobsbawm's approach encourages historians to connect specific historical events and experiences with wider social structures.

He writes:

> “The history of society is thus a collaboration between general models of social structure and change and the specific set of phenomena which actually occurred” (p. 29).

This statement highlights the relationship between general explanations and particular historical evidence.

#### Understanding the quotation

Hobsbawm argues that historical explanation requires both:

1. **General models:** Concepts and frameworks that help historians understand social structures and processes of change.
    
2. **Specific historical phenomena:** The particular events, experiences, and developments that occurred in the past.
    

Neither element is sufficient on its own. General theories need to be examined against historical evidence, while individual events become more meaningful when situated within wider social processes.

#### Hobsbawm and Marxism

The lecture asks students to consider Hobsbawm's position on Marxism.

His approach connects social history with the analysis of social structures and historical change. The quotation on page 29 suggests that theoretical models are important, but they must work in conjunction with the specific historical phenomena being studied.

#### Hobsbawm and the Annales School

The lecture asks students to compare pages 24 and 39 of Hobsbawm's article to describe his relationship with Annales thinking.

The broader connection is the shared interest in moving beyond conventional political history and understanding society through its underlying structures.

However, the lecture does not provide the arguments from pages 24 and 39 themselves. Those passages would need to be consulted to establish Hobsbawm's precise position.

**Key takeaway:** The history of society brings together broader explanations of social structures and change with the specific evidence of historical experience.

#### 8. Important Comparisons for Exams

#### A. Annales School vs. British Marxist historians

**Similarity:** Both challenged conventional political history and examined the structures beneath the surface of society. Both were interested in ordinary people and broader social processes.

**Difference:** The Annales School emphasised geography, regional history, and a more eclectic theoretical approach. British Marxist historians placed greater emphasis on class relations and Marxist interpretations of historical change.

#### B. Braudel vs. Thompson

**Braudel:** Emphasised the long-term structures that shape historical developments. His approach encourages historians to look beyond immediate events.

**Thompson:** Emphasised human experience, working-class agency, and the role of ordinary people in historical change.

**Central distinction:** Braudel's approach highlights the structures within which history unfolds, while Thompson stresses the experiences and actions of historical actors.

#### C. Thompson vs. a purely economic interpretation of industrialisation

A purely economic interpretation might focus on production, growth, and economic organisation.

Thompson's approach examines how economic transformation also affected culture, working habits, and everyday life. It considers how workers experienced, resisted, and participated in these changes.

**Central argument:** Economic transformation and cultural change are interconnected.

#### D. Gramsci vs. a purely coercive understanding of power

A purely coercive understanding focuses on the use of force to maintain dominance.

Gramsci's concept of cultural hegemony draws attention to how dominant groups also maintain power through ideas and cultural assumptions.

**Central argument:** Power operates through culture and ideology as well as through direct coercion.

#### E. Social history vs. the history of society

Social history investigates social groups, relationships, and experiences.

The history of society, as presented in Hobsbawm's article, seeks to connect specific historical phenomena with broader models of social structure and change.

**Central argument:** Historical explanation requires both general models and particular historical evidence.
